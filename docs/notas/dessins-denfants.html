1. The correspondence and its purpose

Notebook page 1 · [G, Introduction and §§1–2]

A dessin d’enfant is, roughly, a finite bipartite graph drawn on a compact oriented surface so that the complementary regions are open discs. The drawing remembers both the graph and the cyclic arrangement of its edges at each vertex. An abstract graph alone does not contain all this information.

The subject becomes arithmetic through several equivalent descriptions of the same object. For connected dessins, the principal descriptions are:

The dictionary used throughout these notes.
ViewpointData to retain
TopologyA connected bipartite graph cellularly embedded in a compact oriented surface without boundary.
CombinatoricsA finite nonempty set $D$ with a transitive action generated by two permutations $\sigma,\alpha$.
Covering spacesA connected finite cover of $\mathbb P^1(\mathbb C)\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}$, or its ramified compactification.
Complex analysisA compact connected Riemann surface $S$ with a nonconstant meromorphic map $\beta:S\to\mathbb P^1$ branched only over $0,1,\infty$.
Function fieldsA finite extension of $\mathbb C(x)$ unramified away from $0,1,\infty$; equivalently its model over $\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)$.
Algebraic geometryA smooth projective connected curve over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ equipped with a Belyi map.

Passing from a surface to its meromorphic functions reverses arrows, so the field-theoretic correspondences are anti-equivalences. If disconnected objects are allowed, replace fields by finite products of fields, namely finite étale algebras.

The arithmetic motivation

Write $G_{\mathbb Q}=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb Q}/\mathbb Q)$. An element of this group acts on the coefficients of a curve and its Belyi map. Transporting this operation through the dictionary gives an action on isomorphism classes of dessins:

\[ G_{\mathbb Q}\curvearrowright \{\text{isomorphism classes of dessins}\}. \]

This action is faithful: an element acting trivially on every dessin is the identity. This is a theorem, not a consequence of merely having an action. The hope is that concrete drawings and finite permutation groups reveal information about the much less explicit group $G_{\mathbb Q}$.

A related construction gives a faithful outer action on the profinite free group on two generators. The Grothendieck–Teichmüller groups organize constraints on this action. We return to the distinction between $\widehat{\mathrm{GT}}$ and the larger $\widehat{\mathrm{GT}}_0$ in §12.

A qualification about the roadmap

The correspondence is not between arbitrary compact Riemann surfaces and dessins. The surface must come with a specified Belyi map. Likewise, a faithful action on a set gives an embedding into its permutation group; obtaining an embedding into a Grothendieck–Teichmüller group requires additional structure and theorems.

2. Historical orientation

Notebook page 2 · [G, Introduction]

Earlier authors often called suitably embedded graphs maps: the complementary discs resemble regions on a geographical map. Regular maps, whose automorphism groups have maximal size, received particular attention.

  • 1957 — Coxeter and Moser. Their book on generators and relations discusses maps and their symmetry groups.
  • 1978 — Jones and Singerman. Their treatment connects maps on orientable surfaces with permutation data and compact Riemann surfaces.
  • 1972–1984 — Grothendieck’s programme. Guillot places the development of the Esquisse d’un programme in this period. Grothendieck’s term dessins d’enfants emphasizes the unexpectedly arithmetic significance of simple drawings.
  • 1979 — Belyi’s theorem. A smooth projective connected complex curve can be defined over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ if and only if it admits a nonconstant map to $\mathbb P^1$ branched over at most three specified points, which may be taken to be $0,1,\infty$.
  • 1990 — Drinfeld. The Grothendieck–Teichmüller group arises in work involving braided categories and related algebraic structures.
  • 1994 — Ihara. The embedding $G_{\mathbb Q}\hookrightarrow\widehat{\mathrm{GT}}$ is established.
  • 1997 — Wolfart. Guillot highlights Wolfart’s rigorous treatment of the direction of Belyi’s theorem often informally called the “obvious” direction.
  • Operadic developments — Fresse. The notebook also records the interpretation of Grothendieck–Teichmüller groups through operads. Guillot’s bibliography lists the relevant monograph as work in preparation, so no publication date is assigned here.

The direction “a three-point branched cover descends to $\overline{\mathbb Q}$” should not be treated as self-evident. Conversely, producing a Belyi map on a curve defined over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ is the other direction of the theorem. The notebook’s reference to Weil-type rigidity belongs to the broader descent story; Guillot emphasizes that his chosen treatment avoids invoking Weil’s rigidity criterion.

3. Bipartite graphs and their realization

Notebook page 3 · [G, §1.1]

Definition 3.1 — A bigraph

A bipartite graph, or bigraph, consists of sets $B,W,D$ and incidence maps

\[ \mathsf B:D\longrightarrow B,\qquad \mathsf W:D\longrightarrow W. \]

The elements of $B$ are black vertices, those of $W$ are white vertices, and those of $D$ are called darts. A dart joins its black endpoint $\mathsf B(d)$ to its white endpoint $\mathsf W(d)$.

Multiple darts may have the same pair of endpoints, and isolated vertices are allowed at this stage. The degree of a vertex is the size of the corresponding incidence-map fibre. The general theory assumes these degrees are finite; the compact objects used later have finite sets $B,W,D$.

The notebook’s example

\[ B=\{b_1,b_2\},\quad W=\{w_1,w_2,w_3\},\quad D=\{d_1,d_2,d_3,d_4\}. \]
DartBlack endpointWhite endpoint
$d_1$$b_1$$w_2$
$d_2$$b_2$$w_2$
$d_3$$b_2$$w_3$
$d_4$$b_2$$w_3$

Thus $\deg(b_1)=1$, $\deg(b_2)=3$, $\deg(w_1)=0$, and $\deg(w_2)=\deg(w_3)=2$. The handwritten list of white vertices omitted $w_3$, although it appears in the drawing; it has been restored.

Morphisms

A morphism $\mathcal G\to\mathcal G'$ consists of maps $f_B:B\to B'$, $f_W:W\to W'$, and $\Delta:D\to D'$ satisfying

\[ \mathsf B'\circ\Delta=f_B\circ\mathsf B,\qquad \mathsf W'\circ\Delta=f_W\circ\mathsf W. \]

These equations say precisely that endpoints are respected. An isomorphism is an invertible morphism; equivalently, all three maps are bijections.

The topological realization

Take one copy $I_d=[0,1]$ for each dart and give $B$ and $W$ the discrete topology. Form

\[ |\mathcal G|= \left(B\sqcup W\sqcup\bigsqcup_{d\in D}I_d\right)\big/\!\sim, \qquad 0_d\sim\mathsf B(d),\quad 1_d\sim\mathsf W(d), \]

with the quotient topology. Thus every dart becomes an interval with the prescribed endpoints. A bigraph isomorphism induces a homeomorphism of its realization.

Ordinary graphs as bigraphs

Insert a white vertex into every edge of an ordinary graph. Every white vertex now has degree two, and the two incident darts together form one original edge. In this convention a graph is a bigraph whose white vertices all have degree two. The degree-two requirement is on the white vertices, not on every vertex. A dessin with this property is called clean.

4. Faces, cell complexes, and triangles

Notebook pages 3–4 · [G, §§1.2–1.5]

Attaching faces

A boundary walk on a bigraph is an even tuple $(d_1,\ldots,d_{2m})$ tracing an alternating closed path. With indices interpreted cyclically, the incidence conditions are

\[ \mathsf W(d_{2i-1})=\mathsf W(d_{2i}),\qquad \mathsf B(d_{2i})=\mathsf B(d_{2i+1}) \quad(1\leq i\leq m). \]

Darts may repeat. In particular, $(d,d)$ is a legitimate boundary walk. A cell complex $\mathcal C=(\mathcal G,F,\partial)$ consists of a bigraph, a set of faces $F$, and a prescribed boundary walk $\partial f$ for every face. Its realization $|\mathcal C|$ is obtained by attaching a closed disc for each face along that walk.

Proposition 4.1 — Connectedness and compactness

For these cell complexes, $|\mathcal C|$ is connected if and only if $|\mathcal G|$ is connected. Moreover, $|\mathcal C|$ is compact if and only if $B,W,D,F$ are all finite.

Expanded proof. Connectedness. The attaching image of each face boundary is connected and therefore lies in one graph component. Attaching its disc cannot join different components, and cannot disconnect the component to which it is attached. Thus the components of the graph and complex correspond. Equivalently, cellular approximation moves paths into the one-skeleton.

Compactness. If there are finitely many cells, the realization is a quotient of a finite disjoint union of compact discs, intervals, and points. Conversely, suppose there are infinitely many cells. Choose a point in the interior of each of infinitely many distinct cells. Every closed cell meets only finitely many of these chosen points, because a CW complex is closure-finite. By the weak topology, every subset of the chosen set is closed. The chosen set is therefore an infinite closed discrete subspace, which cannot be a closed subspace of a compact space. This proves the converse.

□

Canonical triangles

Choose a centre $\star_f$ in each face and join it to every vertex occurrence in its boundary. A face with boundary length $2m$ yields $2m$ triangles, one for each dart occurrence. Each triangle has three vertex types: black $\bullet$, white $\circ$, and centre $\star$.

Let $T$ be the indexing set of these triangles. There are maps

\[ \mathsf D:T\to D,\qquad \mathsf F:T\to F. \]

Different occurrences of the same dart must remain distinct in $T$. This subdivision need not be a simplicial complex: two triangles can have the same vertices without being the same triangle.

InvolutionSide crossedWhat stays fixed
$a$$\star-\bullet$The face centre and black vertex.
$b$$\bullet-\circ$The dart and its endpoints.
$c$$\star-\circ$The face centre and white vertex.

The involutions $a$ and $c$ are defined within faces. For a surface, a dart has two incident triangle occurrences, or one if it lies on the boundary. The involution $b$ exchanges the two occurrences; at a boundary dart it fixes the sole occurrence.

When does the complex form a surface?

For a surface without boundary, every vertex must have positive degree, every dart must occur exactly twice in the face boundaries counting multiplicity, and the link at every vertex must be connected. Equivalently, the graph of consecutive incident darts at each vertex must be connected. Allowing one or two occurrences per dart gives the version with boundary.

The link condition excludes several sheets meeting only at one vertex. Incidence counts alone cannot exclude that singularity. A complex satisfying the surface condition is a dessin in Guillot’s broad topological terminology.

Theorem 4.2 — Reconstruction from triangle data

Let $T$ be a finite set with involutions $a,b,c$, where $a$ and $c$ have no fixed points. Then this data reconstructs a compact dessin, possibly with boundary, up to a unique isomorphism compatible with the given identification of its triangles with $T$. It has no boundary precisely when $b$ also has no fixed points.

Construction. Take one labelled triangle for each element of $T$ and glue corresponding sides according to $a,b,c$. The vertex, dart, and face sets can be recovered as orbit sets:

\[ \begin{aligned} B&=T/\langle a,b\rangle,& W&=T/\langle b,c\rangle,\\ D&=T/\langle b\rangle,& F&=T/\langle a,c\rangle. \end{aligned} \]

The alternating $a,c$-walk in each face orbit gives its boundary. A fixed point of $b$ leaves the corresponding dart unpaired on the surface boundary. The orbit descriptions force every compatible map on the remaining data once its map on $T$ is specified. This is the meaning of uniqueness here: an unlabelled dessin may still have nontrivial automorphisms.

□

Morphisms and the face-lifting statement

A cell-complex morphism consists of a bigraph morphism and a triangle map $\Theta:T\to T'$ satisfying

\[ \mathsf D'\Theta=\Delta\mathsf D,\qquad \Theta(t^a)=\Theta(t)^{a'},\qquad \Theta(t^c)=\Theta(t)^{c'}. \]

For closed dessins it is equivalently a map of triangle sets commuting with all three involutions. The notebook records a useful lifting principle: when every face boundary has pairwise distinct darts, a triangle is determined by its pair $(\mathsf F(t),\mathsf D(t))$. Thus prescribed face and dart maps determine at most one triangle map, necessarily satisfying

\[ \mathsf F'(\Theta(t))=\Phi(\mathsf F(t)),\qquad \mathsf D'(\Theta(t))=\Delta(\mathsf D(t)). \]

This lift is a morphism when the boundary data also respect the $a$- and $c$-adjacencies. That compatibility is included explicitly here.

Why matching boundary sets is not enough

The handwritten “lax morphism” shorthand, following [G, Lemma 1.11], suppresses an adjacency issue. Take one black vertex, two white vertices, and a face with boundary $(d_1,d_2,d_3,d_4)$, where the first two darts meet the first white vertex and the last two the second. All four darts are distinct. Swapping $d_1,d_2$ and fixing $d_3,d_4$ preserves the bigraph and the boundary-dart set, but does not preserve the black-side pairings $(d_2,d_3)$ and $(d_4,d_1)$. The forced triangle map therefore fails to commute with $a$. Distinctness gives uniqueness of a candidate lift; adjacency compatibility gives its existence as a morphism.

5. Orientations and permutation descriptions

Notebook pages 4–5 · [G, §§1.6–1.8]

We use right actions: $t^{gh}=(t^g)^h$, so the leftmost permutation is applied first. This convention is important when multiplying permutations.

Proposition 5.1 — Orientation by triangle colours

A compact connected dessin is orientable if and only if its triangles can be coloured black and white so that distinct triangles sharing a side have different colours. There are exactly two such colourings in the connected orientable case.

The black/white colouring of triangles is different from the black/white colouring of vertices. Every triangle already has one vertex of each of the types $\star,\bullet,\circ$.

Expanded proof for a surface without boundary. Orient every triangle by the same type ordering $\star\to\bullet\to\circ$, and orient each edge type accordingly. The two triangles meeting along an edge induce the same reference direction on that edge. A cellular chain $z=\sum_{t\in T}n_t\,t$ therefore has zero boundary exactly when adjacent triangle coefficients are opposite:

\[ n_{t^a}=n_{t^b}=n_{t^c}=-n_t. \]

From orientation to colours. A compact connected closed surface is orientable precisely when $H_2(|\mathcal C|;\mathbb Z)\ne0$. A nonzero two-cycle has at least one nonzero coefficient. Triangle adjacency is connected, so the displayed equations force every coefficient to equal either that coefficient or its negative. Colour the triangles according to its sign.

From colours to orientation. Give black triangles coefficient $+1$ and white triangles coefficient $-1$. Every interior edge cancels in the boundary, producing a nonzero two-cycle. Since there are no three-cells, it represents a nonzero class in $H_2$. Finally, fixing one triangle colour determines all the others; reversing every colour gives the only other choice.

□

The boundary qualification

For a compact connected surface with nonempty boundary, $H_2(S;\mathbb Z)=0$ even when $S$ is orientable. The corresponding criterion uses $H_2(S,\partial S;\mathbb Z)$, or compatible local orientations directly. A fixed point of $b$ represents a boundary side; it does not require a triangle to have a colour different from itself.

An orientation-preserving morphism sends black triangles to black triangles and white triangles to white triangles. Maps of black and white vertices are already part of the bigraph structure and alone do not express preservation of surface orientation.

From triangles to darts

Now suppose the dessin is oriented and has no boundary. Every dart belongs to exactly two triangle occurrences, one of each colour. Hence it is in bijection with the set $T_+$ of black triangles. The products

\[ \sigma=ab,\qquad \alpha=bc,\qquad \phi=ca \]

preserve $T_+$ and thus act on the dart set $D$. Since $a^2=b^2=c^2=1$,

\[ \sigma\alpha\phi=(ab)(bc)(ca)=1,\qquad \phi=(\sigma\alpha)^{-1}. \]

The cycles of $\sigma$ describe rotation around black vertices; those of $\alpha$ describe rotation around white vertices. The cycles of $\phi$ describe successive black triangles around faces. Consequently, $\sigma$ and $\alpha$ determine the entire triple.

Theorem 5.2 — Reconstruction from two permutations

A finite set $D$ with permutations $\sigma,\alpha$ determines a compact oriented dessin without boundary, unique up to an orientation-preserving isomorphism compatible with the given identification of its darts. Its third permutation is $\phi=(\sigma\alpha)^{-1}$. The dessin is connected if and only if $\langle\sigma,\alpha\rangle$ acts transitively on $D$.

Explicit construction added for this edition. Put $T=D\times\{+,-\}$ and define

\[ \begin{aligned} (d,+)^a&=(d^\sigma,-),& (d,-)^a&=(d^{\sigma^{-1}},+),\\ (d,+)^b&=(d,-),& (d,-)^b&=(d,+),\\ (d,+)^c&=(d^{\alpha^{-1}},-),& (d,-)^c&=(d^\alpha,+). \end{aligned} \]

Each map is a fixed-point-free involution: it changes the sign, and applying it twice applies a permutation followed by its inverse. Theorem 4.2 constructs the closed surface. Colour the $+$ triangles black and the $-$ triangles white. On the $+$ triangles, $ab$ induces $\sigma$, $bc$ induces $\alpha$, and $ca$ induces $\alpha^{-1}\sigma^{-1}=\phi$ in the right-action convention.

Triangle adjacency components are precisely the $\langle a,b,c\rangle$-orbits. Their black triangles form precisely the $\langle\sigma,\alpha\rangle$-orbits, giving the connectedness criterion.

□

Equivalences of categories

Let $\mathsf{UDessins}$ denote compact dessins without boundary, with no orientation specified; nonorientable surfaces are permitted. Let $\mathsf{Dessins}$ denote compact oriented dessins without boundary, with orientation-preserving morphisms. Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathsf{UDessins}&\simeq\mathsf{Sets}_{a,b,c},\\ \mathsf{Dessins}&\simeq\mathsf{Sets}_{\sigma,\alpha,\phi}. \end{aligned} \]

On the first line, objects are finite sets with three fixed-point-free involutions. On the second, objects are finite sets with permutations satisfying $\sigma\alpha\phi=1$. Morphisms in both cases are maps commuting with the distinguished permutations. Allowing fixed points of $b$ in the first line includes surfaces with boundary.

Reading off the surface

If $n=|D|$ and $c(\rho)$ denotes the number of cycles of a permutation, including one-cycles, then

\[ \chi(|\mathcal C|) =c(\sigma)+c(\alpha)+c(\phi)-n. \]

For a connected dessin this equals $2-2g$, so

\[ g=1+\frac{n-c(\sigma)-c(\alpha)-c(\phi)}2. \]

This formula counts black vertices, white vertices, darts, and faces. For a disconnected dessin, apply it component by component; there is no single connected-surface genus.

Worked example added for this edition

Take $D=\{1,2,3\}$, $\sigma=(123)$, and $\alpha=(12)$. Applying $\sigma$ first and then $\alpha$ gives $\sigma\alpha=(23)$, so $\phi=(23)$. The action is transitive, and the cycle counts are $1,2,2$. Thus $\chi=1+2+2-3=2$, and the surface is a sphere. There is one black vertex of degree three, two white vertices of degrees two and one, and two faces.

6. Three descriptions of isomorphism classes

Notebook page 5 · [G, Proposition 1.24]

Labelled permutations and simultaneous conjugation

Label the $n$ darts by $\{1,\ldots,n\}$. The dessin is described by $(n,G,\sigma,\alpha)$, where $G=\langle\sigma,\alpha\rangle\leq S_n$ is its cartographic, or monodromy, group. Two such data describe isomorphic dessins exactly when $n=n'$ and a single relabelling $q:D\to D'$ satisfies

\[ q(d^\sigma)=q(d)^{\sigma'},\qquad q(d^\alpha)=q(d)^{\alpha'}. \]

This is simultaneous conjugacy of the pair in $S_n$. Conjugating the two permutations independently is not sufficient. A relabelling automatically conjugates the generated group as well.

Finite-index subgroups of a free group

Let $F_2=\langle s,a\rangle$ be the abstract free group on two generators, acting on $D$ through $s\mapsto\sigma$ and $a\mapsto\alpha$. For a connected dessin choose a dart $d_0$, and let $K=\operatorname{Stab}_{F_2}(d_0)$. Then

\[ K\backslash F_2\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }D,\qquad Kg\longmapsto d_0^g,\qquad [F_2:K]=|D|. \]

Expanded verification. If $g'=kg$ with $k\in K$, then $d_0^{g'}=(d_0^k)^g=d_0^g$, so the map is well-defined. If $d_0^g=d_0^{g'}$, then $gg'^{-1}\in K$, hence $Kg=Kg'$, proving injectivity. Transitivity proves surjectivity. Changing the chosen dart to $d_0^u$ replaces its stabilizer by $u^{-1}Ku$.

□

Conversely, a finite-index subgroup gives the finite transitive right action on its left cosets. Therefore

\[ \begin{gathered} \{\text{connected dessins up to isomorphism}\}\\ \longleftrightarrow\\ \{\text{finite-index subgroups of }F_2 \text{ up to conjugacy}\}. \end{gathered} \]

A finite group with a core-free subgroup

A third description uses $(G,\sigma,\alpha,H)$, where $G$ is finite, $\sigma,\alpha$ generate $G$, and $H\leq G$ satisfies

\[ \operatorname{Core}_G(H) :=\bigcap_{g\in G}g^{-1}Hg=\{1\}. \]

The associated dart set is $H\backslash G$ with right multiplication. The kernel of this action is precisely the displayed intersection: an element fixes every coset if and only if it belongs to every conjugate of $H$. The condition is thus exactly faithfulness of the action.

Two quadruples give the same isomorphism class if and only if there exists a group isomorphism $\psi:G\to G'$ with $\psi(\sigma)=\sigma'$, $\psi(\alpha)=\alpha'$, and $\psi(H)$ conjugate to $H'$.

The descriptions fit together. If $q:F_2\twoheadrightarrow G$ sends the free generators to $\sigma,\alpha$, then $K=q^{-1}(H)$, and

\[ \ker q=\operatorname{Core}_{F_2}(K). \]

Connectedness is essential for a single coset space. General finite dessins decompose into connected components, hence into transitive orbits.

7. Ramified covers and monodromy

Notebook page 6 · [G, Theorems 2.1–2.3]

Definition 7.1 — Ramified cover

Let $S,R$ be compact topological surfaces without boundary. A ramified cover $p:S\to R$ is a map which, in suitable charts centred at each $s\in S$ and at $p(s)$, takes the form

\[ z\longmapsto z^{e_s},\qquad e_s\geq1. \]

The integer $e_s$ is the ramification index. The point $s$ is ramified when $e_s>1$.

The ramified points form a finite subset of $S$. Their images form the branch set $R_r\subseteq R$, called the “ramification set” in the notebook and in Guillot’s paper. These two sets lie in different surfaces and should not be confused.

Deleting the branch values and their entire inverse image gives a finite unramified covering:

\[ p:S\setminus p^{-1}(R_r)\longrightarrow R\setminus R_r. \]

Theorem 7.2 — Filling the punctures

Fix a finite set $A\subset R$. The category of finite coverings of $R\setminus A$ is equivalent to the category of compact ramified covers of $R$ whose branch set is contained in $A$. Morphisms are maps over the specified base.

Explanation. Restriction gives one functor. Conversely, a finite connected cover of a punctured disc has the form $z\mapsto z^e$. Adjoin its missing central point and extend by the same power map. Doing this at all punctures compactifies the cover. The extensions are unique up to isomorphism over $R$, and maps between covers extend across the added points. A point of $A$ may turn out to be unramified; the branch set is only required to be contained in $A$.

Theorem 7.3 — The fibre with its monodromy action

If $R$ is connected and $*\in R\setminus A$, taking the fibre at $*$ gives an equivalence between finite covering spaces of $R\setminus A$ and finite right $\pi_1(R\setminus A,*)$-sets:

\[ (p:U\to R\setminus A)\longmapsto p^{-1}(*). \]

A loop acts by lifting it from a point of the fibre and recording the lift’s endpoint. Homotopic based loops give the same result. Concatenating loops agrees with the right-action convention. Connected covering spaces correspond exactly to transitive actions.

The three-punctured sphere

Take $R=\mathbb P^1(\mathbb C)\cong S^2$, $A=\{0,1,\infty\}$, and $*=\tfrac12$. Then

\[ \pi_1(\mathbb P^1\setminus\{0,1,\infty\},*) \cong F_2. \]

Choose generators represented by positive loops about $0$ and $1$. For example, the loops $t\mapsto\frac12e^{2\pi it}$ and $t\mapsto1-\frac12e^{2\pi it}$ are based at $\tfrac12$. Their actions on the fibre are denoted $\sigma$ and $\alpha$. With a compatible based loop about $\infty$, the third permutation satisfies $\sigma\alpha\phi=1$.

Theorem 7.4 — Dessins and three-point covers

The category of compact oriented dessins without boundary is equivalent to the category of ramified covers of $\mathbb P^1$ branched only over $0,1,\infty$.

Indeed, both categories are equivalent to finite right $F_2$-sets. The degree of the cover is the cardinality of its fibre, hence the number of darts.

8. From the drawing to a Belyi pair

Notebook pages 6–7 · [G, Proposition 2.4, Lemma 2.5, and Theorem 2.8]

The geometric construction

In a canonical triangle, send the black vertex to $0$, the white vertex to $1$, and the face centre to $\infty$. Oppositely coloured neighbouring triangles map to the two triangles of a sphere subdivided by the marked points $0,1,\infty$. Gluing compatibly along their sides gives a map $|\mathcal C|\to S^2$.

Around a black vertex the local degree is its valency, and similarly at a white vertex. Around a face centre it is the length of the corresponding $\phi$-cycle, which is half the length of the face boundary walk counted with multiplicity.

Proposition 8.1 — Recovering the original graph

If a dessin $\mathcal C$ corresponds to $p:S\to\mathbb P^1$, there is a homeomorphism $|\mathcal C|\cong S$ taking the embedded bigraph $|\mathcal G|$ onto $p^{-1}([0,1])$.

In particular, the black vertices are $p^{-1}(0)$, the white vertices are $p^{-1}(1)$, and each component of $p^{-1}((0,1))$ is one dart interior. The face centres lie above $\infty$.

A local lemma around a branch value

Lemma 8.2 — One point over the centre in each local sheet

Let $p:S\to R$ be a ramified cover, let $x\in R_r$, and let $U$ be an open disc neighbourhood of $x$ containing no other branch value. Every connected component $V$ of $p^{-1}(U)$ is a disc and contains exactly one point $s$ over $x$. In suitable identifications the following diagram commutes:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} \mathbb D & \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } & V\\ {\scriptstyle z\mapsto z^{e_s}}\big\downarrow && \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle p}\\ \mathbb D & \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } & U . \end{array} \]

Expanded explanation. Remove the centre and pull back the covering to a punctured unit disc $\mathbb D^\times$:

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} E & \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } & p^{-1}(U\setminus\{x\})\\ {\scriptstyle \pi}\big\downarrow && \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle p}\\ \mathbb D^\times & \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } & U\setminus\{x\}. \end{array} \]

Here $E$ is the pullback space, not the complex plane and not necessarily a connected space. Each connected component is a finite connected covering of $\mathbb D^\times$. Since $\pi_1(\mathbb D^\times)\cong\mathbb Z$, such a covering corresponds to $e\mathbb Z$ for some $e\geq1$ and is isomorphic to $z\mapsto z^e$ on the punctured disc.

Each component has a unique ramified filling by one central point. Applying the uniqueness of the filling in Theorem 7.2 identifies that filled disc with a component of $p^{-1}(U)$. It contains precisely one point over $x$, and the extension is the displayed power map.

□

The complex structure

If $R$ is a Riemann surface, a topological ramified cover $p:S\to R$ determines a unique complex structure on $S$ making $p$ holomorphic. Away from the ramified points, pull back charts along local inverse branches. Near a ramified point, use the coordinate in which $p$ is $z\mapsto z^e$. Morphisms over $R$ are then holomorphic.

Definition 8.3 — Belyi map and Belyi pair

A Belyi map is a nonconstant holomorphic map $\beta:S\to\mathbb P^1$ from a compact Riemann surface, unramified outside $\{0,1,\infty\}$. If $S$ is disconnected, the map must be nonconstant on every component. The pair $(S,\beta)$ is a Belyi pair.

A holomorphic map to $\mathbb P^1$ is the same thing as a meromorphic function. Combining this fact with the preceding correspondence gives

\[ \mathsf{Dessins}\simeq\mathsf{Belyi}. \]

A morphism $(S,\beta)\to(S',\beta')$ is a holomorphic map $h:S\to S'$ satisfying $\beta'\circ h=\beta$. Forgetting $\beta$ loses essential information: one curve can carry many different dessins.

A concrete example added for this edition

The map $\beta(z)=z^n$ on $\mathbb P^1$, with $n\geq2$, branches at $0$ and $\infty$. Its dessin $\beta^{-1}([0,1])$ has a central black vertex at $0$, $n$ white vertices at the $n$th roots of unity, and $n$ radial darts. Here $\sigma$ is an $n$-cycle, $\alpha$ is the identity, and $\phi=\sigma^{-1}$. Thus $1+n+1-n=2$, as expected for the sphere.

9. Meromorphic functions and étale algebras

Notebook pages 7–8 · [G, §2.4]

For a compact connected Riemann surface $S$, let $\mathcal M(S)$ be its field of meromorphic functions. If $S=S_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup S_r$ has several connected components, then

\[ \mathcal M(S)=\prod_{i=1}^r\mathcal M(S_i). \]

This is generally an algebra rather than a field. A finite étale algebra over a field $K$ is a finite product of finite separable field extensions of $K$. The separability condition is automatic in characteristic zero, which is the setting here.

Theorem 9.1 — Covers and meromorphic function algebras

Fix a compact connected Riemann surface $R$. The category of compact Riemann surfaces equipped with a finite ramified cover of $R$ is anti-equivalent to the category of finite étale $\mathcal M(R)$-algebras. The assignment is $S\mapsto\mathcal M(S)$, and

\[ \deg(S\to R)= \dim_{\mathcal M(R)}\mathcal M(S). \]

This is a classical theorem about compact Riemann surfaces, quoted here as in the notebook. A covering map $p:S\to R$ makes $\mathcal M(S)$ an $\mathcal M(R)$-algebra by pullback $f\mapsto f\circ p$.

Why the arrows reverse

A map $h:S\to S'$ over $R$ gives

\[ h^*:\mathcal M(S')\longrightarrow\mathcal M(S), \qquad f\longmapsto f\circ h. \]

Thus the map of algebras points in the opposite direction. “Anti-equivalent” means precisely “equivalent to the opposite category,” not merely that the objects are in bijection.

The base sphere and its valuations

For the sphere, $\mathcal M(\mathbb P^1)=\mathbb C(x)$. The element $x$ is the coordinate on the target. Under the inclusion into $\mathcal M(S)$, it becomes the meromorphic function $p$.

For $a\in\mathbb C$, the point $a$ determines the order-of-vanishing valuation at $x-a$; at $\infty$, use the local parameter $1/x$. If $s\in S$ lies over $a$, then in suitable local coordinates the pullback of a parameter at $a$ has order $e_s$. Consequently, analytic ramification and algebraic ramification of these valuations agree.

Lemma 9.2 — Geometric and algebraic branch points

For a connected ramified cover $p:S\to\mathbb P^1$ whose function-field extension is Galois, a point $a\in\mathbb P^1$ belongs to the branch set if and only if $\mathcal M(S)/\mathbb C(x)$ ramifies at the valuation belonging to $a$.

For a general separable extension, “unramified at $a$” means that every valuation above $a$ has ramification index one. For a finite product of extensions, every factor must satisfy the condition. Section 10 explains why this agrees with testing a Galois closure in our setting.

Two uses of “étale”

A finite separable extension is an étale algebra over the generic field $\mathbb C(x)$, even if the corresponding compact curve cover has branch points. The extra phrase “unramified outside $\{0,1,\infty\}$” controls valuations at points of the base curve; it is not automatic from being a finite étale algebra over its function field.

Worked computation added for this edition

For $p(z)=z^n$, the inclusion is $\mathbb C(x)\hookrightarrow\mathbb C(z)$ with $x\mapsto z^n$. The extension has degree $n$, since $T^n-x$ is irreducible over $\mathbb C(x)$ by Eisenstein at $x$ in $\mathbb C[x]$. Its automorphisms are $z\mapsto\zeta z$ for $\zeta^n=1$.

At $z=0$, the parameter $x$ pulls back to $z^n$, so the ramification index is $n$. At infinity, $1/x=(1/z)^n$, giving the same index. At finite nonzero points, the derivative $nz^{n-1}$ does not vanish, so there is no ramification. This matches the star-shaped dessin in §8.

10. Regular covers and the Galois closure

Notebook page 8 · [G, Lemma 2.12, Theorem 2.13, and §3]

A connected covering of degree $n$ is regular when it has $n$ automorphisms over its base. An automorphism of a connected covering is determined by the image of one point in a fibre, so there are at most $n$ such automorphisms. Having $n$ means that the automorphism group acts transitively, and freely, on that fibre.

Under the subgroup classification, a connected cover corresponding to $K\leq\pi_1$ is regular precisely when $K$ is normal. Under the function-field correspondence, regularity is equivalent to the extension being Galois.

Lemma 10.1 — The Galois closure has the same branch set

Let $p:S\to\mathbb P^1$ be a connected ramified cover. Let $\widetilde p:\widetilde S\to\mathbb P^1$ correspond to the Galois closure of $\mathcal M(S)/\mathbb C(x)$. Then

\[ \operatorname{Br}(p)=\operatorname{Br}(\widetilde p). \]

Expanded proof. The first inclusion. The field inclusion gives a factorization $\widetilde S\to S\to\mathbb P^1$. Ramification indices multiply in a tower. If $p$ is ramified over a point, then so is the composite. Hence $\operatorname{Br}(p)\subseteq\operatorname{Br}(\widetilde p)$.

The reverse inclusion. Put $A=\operatorname{Br}(p)$ and restrict to an unramified covering $U\to\mathbb P^1\setminus A$. If this covering corresponds to $K\leq\pi_1(\mathbb P^1\setminus A,*)$, its minimal regular covering above it corresponds to

\[ N=\bigcap_{g\in\pi_1}g^{-1}Kg. \]

This is a finite-index normal subgroup: it is the kernel of the finite permutation action on $K\backslash\pi_1$. Its covering is still unramified on $\mathbb P^1\setminus A$. Compactifying it by Theorem 7.2 creates possible branch points only in $A$.

The compactified function-field extension is Galois and contains $\mathcal M(S)$. Moreover, $N$ is the largest normal subgroup contained in $K$, so it gives the smallest such regular cover above $U$. Thus its function field is the Galois closure, and the compactification is $\widetilde S$. Therefore $\operatorname{Br}(\widetilde p)\subseteq A$.

□

The statement concerns branch values in $\mathbb P^1$. The two covering surfaces have different sets of points, so one should not literally equate their sets of ramified points upstairs.

Theorem 10.2 — The function-field form of dessins

Let $\mathsf{Et}_{0,1,\infty}(\mathbb C(x))$ denote finite étale $\mathbb C(x)$-algebras unramified outside $\{0,1,\infty\}$, with unital $\mathbb C(x)$-algebra homomorphisms. Then

\[ \mathsf{Dessins}^{\mathrm{op}} \simeq\mathsf{Et}_{0,1,\infty}(\mathbb C(x)). \]

Theorem 9.1 identifies the algebras with covers; the compatibility of ramification and Lemma 10.1 impose exactly the branch restriction needed for dessins. Connected dessins correspond to fields, and regular connected dessins correspond to Galois extensions.

11. Descent to the algebraic numbers

Notebook page 8 · [G, §2.5, Lemmas 2.14 and 2.16, Theorems 2.15 and 2.17]

Fix the inclusion $\overline{\mathbb Q}\subset\mathbb C$. The field $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ consists of all complex numbers algebraic over $\mathbb Q$, not just the rational numbers themselves.

Definition 11.1 — An extension defined over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$

Let $L/\mathbb C(x)$ be finite Galois of degree $n$. It is defined over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ if there exists a subfield $L_{\mathrm{rat}}\subset L$ containing $\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)$, Galois over it, such that

\[ [L_{\mathrm{rat}}:\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)] =[L:\mathbb C(x)]=n. \]

Equivalently, extending the base field from $\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)$ to $\mathbb C(x)$ recovers $L$:

\[ L_{\mathrm{rat}}\otimes_{\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)} \mathbb C(x)\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }L, \qquad u\otimes f\longmapsto uf. \]

The subscript “rat” names the arithmetic model. It does not mean “the elements which are rational numbers.” In particular, $L_{\mathrm{rat}}$ contains the transcendental element $x$ and algebraic functions of $x$. What descends is the extension over the specified base, or geometrically the covering map.

What extending constants means

One can also write

\[ L\cong \operatorname{Frac} \left(L_{\mathrm{rat}}\otimes_{\overline{\mathbb Q}}\mathbb C\right). \]

The fraction-field symbol matters. The raw tensor product over the constant field need not already be a field; the tensor product over $\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)$ in Definition 11.1 has the necessary rational functions in its base.

Correction to the handwritten tensor product

The notebook writes $L\cong L_{\mathrm{rat}}\otimes_{\overline{\mathbb Q}}\mathbb C$, following the same shorthand in the reference. For function fields, use one of the two precise formulas above. Already for $L_{\mathrm{rat}}=\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)$, tensoring only over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ does not invert every nonzero polynomial in $\mathbb C[x]$.

For example, take $t\in\mathbb C$ transcendental over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$. Evaluation at $x=t$ is defined on $\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)\otimes_{\overline{\mathbb Q}}\mathbb C$, since no nonzero polynomial over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ vanishes at $t$. It sends the nonzero element $x-t$ to zero. Thus $x-t$ is not a unit there, whereas it is invertible in $\mathbb C(x)$.

Uniqueness and the Galois group

Lemma 11.2 — Uniqueness of the arithmetic subfield

If $L/\mathbb C(x)$ is defined over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$, then the subfield $L_{\mathrm{rat}}\subset L$ satisfying Definition 11.1 is unique.

This is a constant-field descent lemma, quoted rather than proved here. In this setting one can identify $L_{\mathrm{rat}}$ with the relative algebraic closure of $\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)$ in $L$: the elements of $L$ algebraic over $\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)$. The algebraic closedness of $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ is crucial; the same uniqueness statement does not hold for arbitrary pairs of constant fields.

Lemma 11.3 — Restriction preserves the Galois group

For a finite Galois extension defined over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$, restriction gives

\[ \operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb C(x)) \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } \operatorname{Gal} (L_{\mathrm{rat}}/\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)). \]

Explanation of the restriction map. Uniqueness of the arithmetic subfield shows it is preserved by every $\mathbb C(x)$-automorphism of $L$. An automorphism trivial on that subfield and on $\mathbb C(x)$ is trivial on their compositum $L$, so restriction is injective. Conversely, an automorphism of $L_{\mathrm{rat}}$ fixing $\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)$ extends through the tensor-product formula by acting on its first factor and fixing $\mathbb C(x)$. This proves surjectivity.

□

The descent theorem

Theorem 11.4 — Algebraic branch values imply descent

If $L/\mathbb C(x)$ is a finite Galois extension and all its branch values belong to $\overline{\mathbb Q}\cup\{\infty\}$, then the extension is defined over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$.

This is the substantial descent theorem quoted in Guillot as Theorem 2.15, using Völklein’s Theorem 7.9. It is not proved in the handwritten pages, and no elementary proof is asserted here. It applies in particular when the branch set is contained in $\{0,1,\infty\}$.

Removing the Galois hypothesis

Let $K/\mathbb C(x)$ be any finite extension unramified outside $\{0,1,\infty\}$, and let $L$ be its Galois closure. By Lemma 10.1, $L$ has the same branch set and hence descends. Put

\[ H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K),\qquad K_{\mathrm{rat}}=L_{\mathrm{rat}}^{\,H}, \]

where $H$ acts on $L_{\mathrm{rat}}$ via Lemma 11.3. Galois correspondence and extension of constants give

\[ K_{\mathrm{rat}}\otimes_{\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)} \mathbb C(x)\cong K. \]

Finite products are handled one factor at a time. Thus the descent is not restricted to regular dessins.

Theorem 11.5 — The arithmetic form of the dictionary

Extension of scalars gives an equivalence

\[ \mathsf{Et}_{0,1,\infty}(\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)) \simeq \mathsf{Et}_{0,1,\infty}(\mathbb C(x)), \]

and consequently

\[ \mathsf{Dessins}^{\mathrm{op}} \simeq \mathsf{Et}_{0,1,\infty}(\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)). \]

Essential surjectivity. Every object on the complex side comes from the arithmetic side by the descent and Galois-closure arguments above.

Full faithfulness. For arithmetic algebras $A,B$, the natural map

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Hom}_{\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)}(A,B) &\longrightarrow \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb C(x)}(A_{\mathbb C},B_{\mathbb C}),\\ A_{\mathbb C}&= A\otimes_{\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)}\mathbb C(x) \end{aligned} \]

is a bijection. Faithfulness follows from the injectivity of scalar extension. For field factors, a map after scalar extension sends elements algebraic over $\overline{\mathbb Q}(x)$ to elements with the same property, hence restricts to the unique arithmetic subfields. Its scalar extension is the original map because the complex fields are generated by those subfields and $\mathbb C(x)$. For products, a unital map is determined by its component maps and its action on the component idempotents. Those idempotents already lie in the arithmetic product. This gives fullness as well.

Example: a model over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$

For $x=z^n$, take $L=\mathbb C(z)$ and $L_{\mathrm{rat}}=\overline{\mathbb Q}(z)$. Both extensions of their respective $x$-fields have degree $n$ and Galois group $\mu_n$. Every $n$th root of unity lies in $\overline{\mathbb Q}$, so each automorphism $z\mapsto\zeta z$ already exists in the arithmetic model.

12. The Grothendieck–Teichmüller outlook

Notebook pages 1–2 · [G, Introduction and §5]

The dictionary explains how $G_{\mathbb Q}$ acts on dessins: represent the dessin as a curve with a Belyi map over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$, apply a Galois automorphism to the coefficients, and return to the corresponding dessin. The coefficient action preserves algebraic equations and the marked branch values $0,1,\infty$.

Its faithfulness, stated in §1, is proved later in the reference. The notebook uses that result as motivation; the present eight-page material builds the correspondence on which that proof relies.

The distinction between the two groups

The embeddings discussed in the introduction fit into

\[ G_{\mathbb Q}\hookrightarrow \widehat{\mathrm{GT}}\hookrightarrow \widehat{\mathrm{GT}}_0 \subseteq\operatorname{Out}(\widehat F_2). \]

The full group $\widehat{\mathrm{GT}}$ satisfies an additional pentagon condition. Guillot’s elementary construction establishes an embedding into the larger group $\widehat{\mathrm{GT}}_0$. In his notation, the unhatted group $\mathrm{GT}$ is isomorphic to $\widehat{\mathrm{GT}}_0$; it should not be silently identified with $\widehat{\mathrm{GT}}$.

The finite groups $H_n$

To make the notebook’s statement “understanding $H_n$ means understanding finite two-generator groups” precise, set

\[ F_2^{(n)} =\bigcap_{\substack{N\trianglelefteq F_2\\{}[F_2:N]\leq n}}N, \qquad H_n=F_2/F_2^{(n)}. \]

There are only finitely many subgroups of each fixed finite index in a finitely generated group: an index-$m$ subgroup produces a coset action on $m$ points, and there are only finitely many choices for the images of finitely many generators in $S_m$. Thus the intersection defining $F_2^{(n)}$ has finite index, and $H_n$ is finite.

Every finite group $G$ of order at most $n$ with a chosen generating pair $(g_1,g_2)$ is a quotient of $H_n$ carrying the distinguished generators to that pair. Indeed, the kernel of $F_2\twoheadrightarrow G$ occurs among the normal subgroups in the displayed intersection.

One concrete construction takes representatives $(G_i,g_{i,1},g_{i,2})$ of all such groups with chosen generating pairs and forms the subgroup

\[ \left\langle (g_{i,1})_i,\ (g_{i,2})_i\right\rangle \ \leq\ \prod_iG_i. \]

This subgroup is $H_n$. The generating pairs are essential: the unmarked abstract group alone does not determine a regular dessin.

Finite approximations and regular dessins

Guillot defines compatible subgroups $\mathrm{GT}(n)\leq\operatorname{Out}(H_n)$ with

\[ \mathrm{GT}=\varprojlim_n\mathrm{GT}(n) \cong\widehat{\mathrm{GT}}_0. \]

The detailed defining conditions belong to the later part of his paper. This statement does not mean that $\mathrm{GT}(n)$ is the entire outer automorphism group, or that the absolute Galois group has been proved equal to the inverse limit.

A finite group $G$ with a distinguished generating pair gives a regular dessin by letting $G$ act on itself on the right. Conversely, the monodromy group of a connected regular dessin acts freely and transitively on its darts. Therefore the finite groups and marked generators entering $H_n$ can be studied through dessins with maximal symmetry. This completes the connection sketched in the opening notebook pages.
